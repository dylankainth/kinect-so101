# Stacking blocks with the SO-101: two days from "it misses" to "within 2 mm"

*2026-10-19 and 2026-10-20. Kinect v1 + SO-101 arm + MuJoCo planner, all on the real hardware.*

The task: put three blocks on top of each other, using a scripted pipeline (Kinect depth for perception, a MuJoCo
inverse-kinematics planner, a safety-wrapped arm driver). This is the first project built on the new `core/` +
`projects/` layout. This post is a record of what we did, what broke, what the data said, and what is left.

**Where it stands:** a two-high stack of flat 8 x 5 x 3 cm boxes works on the real arm, to about 2 mm and 3 degrees
(six trials in a row, none failed). A three-high stack has been built on the real arm but is not yet reliable: the
top layer landed 2.6 cm off once, and the run that was meant to fix it was cut short by a power fault.

---

## 1. Background: how the repo works

The approach is classical and scripted, not learned:

1. **Perceive.** Kinect depth is lifted into the robot frame (x forward, y left, z up, origin at the arm base).
   Anything more than 1.5 cm above the table is grouped, and its top face gives position, yaw and height. It is
   colour-agnostic, so it works on any block.
2. **Plan.** A MuJoCo damped-least-squares IK planner builds the whole pick, carry and place trajectory.
3. **Rehearse (optional).** The same trajectory is played in the sim before the real arm moves.
4. **Execute.** `SafeArm` streams joint targets at 25 Hz, capped at 25 deg/s, and aborts on load, temperature,
   voltage or tracking error. A STOP panel (`core/tools/stop_gui.py`) freezes it.
5. **Look again.** Every step starts with a fresh look at the table. A dropped block shows up as loose and is
   retried. State always comes from the camera, never from what the script believes happened.

Early in the session we also talked through why this is not a VLM/VLA system (a vision-language model would
replace the perception and planning stages, but you would lose the millimetre accuracy the scripted pipeline gets
for free) and what a tic-tac-toe player would need. Short version: use a VLM for "what is on the board / what
should I do", keep the classical planner for "move the piece exactly there".

The restructure into `core/` (control flags, motion, grasp, perception, sim, robot driver, calibration, tools)
and `projects/` (pick_place, stack_blocks, template) was done first and committed (89377cd) without deleting any
code.

## 2. Timeline

### Day 1: build it, then fight the hardware

* **Built `projects/stack_blocks`:** multi-block detection (`core/perception/multi_block.py`), a stack-aware
  planner (`place_z`, `held_width`, `place_hover`, `place_yaw`), a stateless "look, move, look" loop and a
  real-run wrapper (`core/grasp.py`, extracted from the old pick-and-place runner). Tests use fake hardware
  (`tests/`).
* **The blocks were not cubes.** They are 8.0-8.3 x 4.9-5.0 x ~3 cm boxes. The detector had to learn footprints.
* **USB trouble.** The Kinect and the arm's CH343 serial adapter repeatedly dropped off the bus (failed start,
  phantom devices, a hub that could not carry both). Fixes were physical: reseat the Kinect's 12 V adapter, use
  separate ports, a better cable. Windows needs admin to restart a USB device in software.
* **The sim rehearsal said no.** Lying boxes: the swinging jaw lands on the top edge of a 3 cm box, the box
  slides off at release. We stood the boxes on their 8 x 3 cm edge instead. The planner got a hover fallback,
  a tilt retry and a place-yaw ramp to make that reachable.
* **First real motion** (late on day 1): the arm closed on nothing, twice.

### The calibration was 8 cm off

A hover probe (arm held above the block, no grasp) showed the real gripper ~7 cm to the side of where the model
said the grasp point was. The joints were fine (commanded vs measured within 0.5 deg). The camera-to-robot
calibration was stale, so every pick was aimed at the wrong place. `core.calibration.calib_joints` re-fits it from
the arm itself:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| median distance, arm depth points to model, per pose | 38-65 mm | 5-7 mm |
| camera correction | | dy = +83.3 mm, dx = -4.0 mm, dz = -2.7 mm |

The Kinect had moved about 8 cm sideways (most likely nudged when it was replugged). With the fix, the very next
run picked the block first time and built a two-high stack of standing boxes.

### Standing boxes are a dead end

A box standing on its 8 x 3 cm edge is 5 cm tall on a 3 cm footprint. A two-high stack of those has about 7 mm of
tolerance and the arm's accuracy at that point was about 6 mm, so it tipped. A three-high stack of standing boxes
also needs the stack at x >= 0.29 m to be reachable (we mapped it). We tried it: the first layer-2 place on the
far stack ended with the block dragged away from the stack, and we stopped. Lesson: choose the geometry that
tolerates your accuracy.

### The sim cannot referee this

We added a rehearsal loop (`--tune N`, `TUNINGS`) that tries up to seven variants of the release and shows each in
the MuJoCo viewer (`--show-sim`). All seven failed identically, which showed the failure was not in the knobs. A
contact trace explained why: the sim holds the box with the jaws commanded fully closed, which squeezes far
harder than the real gripper does, and the box pivots on the sim's small pads and pops off the stack on release.
The real arm placed boxes that the sim said would fail. The sim is right about reach and collisions and wrong
about contact physics, so the rehearsal gate is now optional (`--skip-rehearsal`, or `--tune 0` for none).

**What "run 1000 sims" would and would not have done:** 1000 variants of the same knobs fail the same way, and
optimising against a sim with these artefacts finds plans that exploit the sim. We chose real trials instead,
because the camera already tells us automatically whether a stack worked.

### Hand-eye auto-calibration was a trap

`core.calibration.handeye_auto` measures where the real jaw tip is. From a camera looking down, the jaw tips are
hidden under the gripper body, so it measured the body about 10 cm up and fitted a **z offset of 89.5 mm**.
Applied by `aim()`, that would have driven every grasp 9 cm into the table. We moved the file aside
(`control/handeye_REJECTED_zoffset_89mm.json`) before anything used it, and the README now warns about it.

### The wrist-roll flip (the real cause of the stack being off)

The first flat-box two-high stack worked but the top box sat 4-5 cm out of position. The joint trace showed why:
over the carry, wrist roll went from **+75 deg at the pick to -83 deg at the place**, a 158 deg turn. The box is
symmetric, so both rolls describe the same orientation, but the box is held off the roll axis, so turning the
wrist nearly 180 deg swung it to the far side of the grasp point by centimetres. The planner chose the roll with
the smallest absolute value; it should choose the equivalent roll *nearest the pick roll*. Now the roll changes by
about 23 deg. One line in `plan_pick_place`, found by reading a trace.

### The release dragged the block off

With standing boxes we saw blocks pulled off the stack as the arm retreated. The fixed pad is still touching the
block when the jaws open, and lifting straight up drags it along. The planner now slides the open gripper 1.5 cm
along the closing axis, away from the fixed pad, before lifting (`release_clear`, `RELEASE_CLEAR`). We also added
a post-release camera snapshot (`control/pre_release.jpg`, `post_release.jpg`, `post_retreat.jpg`) so every
release can be looked at.

### Trials: measure, fit, correct

`projects/stack_blocks/trials.py` stacks two blocks, measures the top block's pose against the base block with the
camera, unstacks it (the arm resets itself), and logs a CSV row per trial.

| | across the base | along the base | yaw |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 trials, no yaw trim | -0.3 to -0.7 mm | -0.9 to +0.7 mm | **+6.9 to +9.8 deg** |
| 9-trial trim sweep (-8.2 / -16.4 / -24.6 deg) | +1.1 +- 0.9 mm | +1.6 +- 2.6 mm | fit: err = 10.3 + 0.87 x trim, 2.7 deg scatter |
| 6 trials at the fitted trim (-11.8 deg) | **+0.3 +- 1.2 mm** (worst 2.0) | **+1.0 +- 1.0 mm** (worst 2.2) | **-0.7 +- 1.4 deg** (worst 3.1) |

Position was already within about 1 mm after the roll and release fixes. Yaw had a consistent +8 deg bias with
about 1 deg of scatter, which is exactly the kind of error you can calibrate out. One early look suggested the
trim did not work (two trials moved only 2 deg for an 8 deg trim); the 9-trial sweep showed that was scatter,
the real response is 0.87 deg per deg. Fit the line, do not trust two points. The trim is now
`PLACE_YAW_TRIM = -11.8 deg` in `projects/stack_blocks/main.py`.

## 3. The three-high attempts

**Attempt 1 (clean run, trim -11.8 deg, flat boxes):** the stack was built and the camera confirmed it: 3 blocks,
top at 9.5 cm. Layer 2 was within 1-2 mm of the base. **Layer 3 landed 2.6 cm off** (1.7 cm further out, 1.8 cm to
the right, ~4 deg of yaw). The planner had used a tool tilt of 0.15 rad for layer 2 and 0.3 rad for layer 3, and
the layer-3 pick was the far-right block (y = +0.129), farther out than anything in the trials. We do not yet know
which of these matters.

**Attempt 2 (with the new `--refine 2` step):** after each place the camera measures the top block against the
layer below; if it is more than 4 mm or 3 deg off, the arm picks it up again and re-places it aimed at
(target - measured error). Layer 2 this time (picked from the left, placed on the middle box) was off by **8.9 mm
(-8.8 mm along x) and -3.5 deg**, much bigger than in the trials, which always moved right to left. So placement
error depends on the direction of the move. The refine step triggered and re-placed the block. Then a servo
reported an *input voltage error* when torque was switched off at the end of the re-place (the weak 5 V supply
sagging), and the run stopped to hold.

**What went wrong next was our own bug.** We released the hold with LIMP and the stacker simply carried on to the
next move, because the new refine code did not check the abort flag. We caught it about 10 s into the layer-3
carry, stopped it, and PARKed. The block fell onto the stack on the way, so the table now has an accidental
three-high stack with a skewed top box. Both causes are fixed (see below); the refine step itself has not yet
been seen to work end to end.

## 4. Safety and tooling fixes made along the way

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| One corrupted temperature packet read 62 C off a 31 C servo and aborted a run | Abort needs **two** hot reads in a row (`TEMP_HOT_READS`), like the voltage check |
| Weak supply sags when torque toggles; "input voltage error" on torque disable | `disable_torque` retries like `enable_torque` already did |
| Refine step ignored a STOP/PARK/fault and started the next move | Abort flag checked after the refine step |
| Camera glitches ("no frames from Kinect") mid-run | Existing restart logic coped; each was logged |
| Switching git branch mid-run deleted the code the run was using and crashed it | Lesson (below); the work was safe in the commit |
| GitHub Desktop staged 12,602 `.venv` files while on `main` | Index reset; `.gitignore` already ignores them on this branch |

New tooling: `--show-sim` (watch each rehearsal in MuJoCo), `--tune N` (try release variants, 0 = none),
`--refine N` (look and re-place), `python -m core.tools.kinect_live` (live camera with the planner's pick and place
targets drawn on it), and `projects/stack_blocks/trials.py` (stack, measure, unstack, log).

## 5. What we learned

1. **Measure before you tune.** The "hand-eye" tool, the sim variants and the first trim guess were all wrong in
   ways a trace or a measurement exposed within minutes. The roll flip and the 8 cm camera error were both found by
   comparing what was commanded with what the camera saw.
2. **Pick the geometry that tolerates your error.** Standing boxes needed 7 mm; flat boxes need centimetres.
3. **The camera is the ground truth, so use it as a sensor, not just a map.** The trial loop and the refine step
   both turn "did it work?" into a number.
4. **Do not trust the sim's physics for contact.** Use it for reach and collision; judge grasping on the real arm.
5. **Calibration drifts.** A bumped Kinect cost hours. A 2-minute `calib_joints` run (clear the mat first) is cheap
   insurance after any physical change.
6. **Do not change the working tree while the arm is running.** Run from a dedicated copy or git worktree, or
   wait for the run to finish before switching branches.
7. **A safety stop should end the run.** Any code path after a fault must check the abort flag; ours did not.

## 6. Next steps

In rough priority order:

1. **Reset the table and rerun three-high with `--refine 2`.** The arm unstacks the accidental stack
   (`trials.unstack(..., level=3)` then `level=2`) and we see whether the refine loop brings every layer within
   about 4 mm / 3 deg. Check the supply first (item 3) so a sag does not end the run.
2. **Find out why error depends on direction and height.** Layer 2 was 2 mm right-to-left and 9 mm left-to-right;
   layer 3 was 26 mm. Extend `trials.py` to log the pick position, tilt, pan and roll change for each move, and to
   run three-high and both directions. Then fit the correction as a function of geometry (for example, trim as a
   function of the roll change) instead of one constant. If the cause is the tool tilt (0.15 vs 0.3 rad), look at
   whether the planner can use a smaller tilt, or compensate for the held-block offset it causes.
3. **Look at the arm's power.** Both the temperature false alarm and the torque-disable fault coincided with a
   weak 5 V supply. Check the adapter's rating, the cable and connector, and consider logging the servo voltage
   during runs so a trend shows up before a fault.
4. **Teach the detector about a stack whose top block is not aligned.** An offset or rotated top block has
   triggered "sees 0 blocks" before, and the parked gripper shows up as "something at (0.16, 0.0)". The refine step
   needs a clean pose of the top block, so give it a dedicated top-face measurement.
5. **Make the sim useful again (optional).** Larger pads and a stiffer box would stop it popping blocks off.
   The cheaper option is to keep the sim for reach and collisions only and drop the physics verdict.
6. **Unattended runs.** The trial loop already resets itself. Add: a cap on consecutive refine attempts,
   temperature-aware pacing, and a clear "stopped, here is why" summary, so it can run for an hour.
7. **Housekeeping.**
   * Add tests for `refine_place` and the abort-after-refine path with the fake hardware.
   * Keep `control/backup_before_recal_1019/` or re-back up `calib.json` and `joint_offsets.json` whenever they
     change; they live in git, so commit them with a message that says why.
   * Run real jobs from a separate worktree.
8. **Beyond stacking.** The same pipeline (look, plan, move, look, correct) supports the other projects we talked
   about. Tic-tac-toe is the natural next one: a vision-language model to read the board and choose a move, and
   this pipeline to place the piece.
